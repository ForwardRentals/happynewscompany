FDA Approves Klygefa, a Once-Weekly Self-Injected Treatment for Generalized Myasthenia Gravis

The nanobody drug from Alexion and AstraZeneca is approved for adults with the most common form of the muscle-weakness disease.

By the Happy News DeskNovember 16, 2026 · 2 min read
AstraZenecaHealth

The US Food and Drug Administration has approved Klygefa (gefurulimab-gaks), a weekly self-injected medicine for adults with generalized myasthenia gravis, a condition in which the immune system disrupts signals between nerves and muscles and causes fluctuating weakness. The approval was announced on November 16, 2026 by Alexion, AstraZeneca's rare disease group.

What was approved

Klygefa is cleared for adults whose disease is positive for anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies, the largest subgroup of patients. It blocks complement C5, a part of the immune system involved in the muscle damage, and is given once a week with an autoinjector, so patients can treat themselves at home. Earlier C5 inhibitors for this condition are given by intravenous infusion.

The evidence

The approval rests on PREVAIL, a phase 3 trial that randomized 260 adults to the drug or a placebo for 26 weeks across 113 sites in 20 countries.

  • Daily-activity score (MG-ADL): improved by 4.2 points on the drug versus 2.6 on placebo, a 1.6-point difference
  • Muscle strength score (QMG): improved by 4.5 versus 2.4 points
  • Responders: 46.3% of drug-treated patients had a QMG improvement of at least 5 points, versus 25.2% on placebo
  • Speed: benefit was seen as early as week one

Why it matters

Roughly 98,500 people in the US are estimated to live with generalized myasthenia gravis, and about 81,000 of them are antibody-positive, according to NeurologyLive. A treatment that can be taken at home rather than in an infusion centre may give many patients more flexibility.

Important context

The label carries a boxed warning for serious meningococcal infections, a known risk of this drug class, and patients must be vaccinated against meningococcus at least two weeks before starting. Across the full development program, two patients died of serious infections. Injection-site reactions were more common on the drug (9.9% versus 3.1%). Patients should talk with their own clinicians about whether a treatment is suitable for them.

Source: NeurologyLive
