A Filter Inspired by Water-Repelling Plants Is Catching Microplastics at an Atlantic City Wastewater Plant

Two Princeton-trained founders' floating PolyPod units now sit in a treated-water basin before it reaches the ocean, with lab tests showing 98% removal in a prototype.

By the Happy News DeskOctober 28, 2026 · 2 min read

Photo: Farragutful / CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons

At a wastewater facility in Atlantic City, New Jersey, a new kind of filter is quietly pulling microplastics out of treated water before it flows to the Atlantic Ocean. The Atlantic County Utilities Authority (ACUA) installed two PolyPod units from the startup PolyGone Systems in October 2026, according to the authority's project page.

How it works

The design copies a trick from nature: certain plants repel water but attract tiny plastic particles. PolyGone's bristle-like filter is hydrophobic, so microplastics stick to it passively as water moves past, with no chemicals needed. The units sit in ACUA's retention basin of treated effluent.

  • Captured plastics are concentrated on site into sludge, then incinerated, per ACUA; the company is also exploring upcycling options.
  • Laboratory tests of a prototype removed 98% of microplastic particles, Good News Network reported on 28 October.
  • The company was founded by Princeton-trained designers Nathanial Banks and Yidian Liu.
"I think this really marks a new era for treatment innovation," said Banks, PolyGone's CEO.

Why it matters

Microplastics enter wastewater through laundry, runoff and litter, and conventional plants were not built to remove them. ACUA president Matt DeNafo noted they end up in fish people eat, calling it a human-health issue.

Context and caveats

  • The 98% figure comes from lab work; performance at commercial scale is what the Atlantic City deployment will show.
  • Coverage differed on whether the system is still in a pilot phase, and "world's first" is ACUA's and the company's own description.
  • No federal rule currently requires utilities to remove microplastics, so ACUA is acting voluntarily.
  • Incinerating the captured sludge is an imperfect end point, which is why upcycling is being tested.
Source: Atlantic County Utilities Authority
