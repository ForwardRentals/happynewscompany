Wolbachia Mosquitoes Cut Dengue Infections by 68% in a Brazilian Trial of 5,757 Children

Early results from a randomized trial in Belo Horizonte also showed a 73% drop in chikungunya, though the findings are not yet peer-reviewed.

By the Happy News DeskNovember 21, 2026 · 2 min read

Photo: Wee Hong / CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons

Releasing mosquitoes carrying a harmless bacterium called Wolbachia cut dengue infections among schoolchildren in Belo Horizonte, Brazil, by about 68%, according to results presented on 21 November 2026 at the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene meeting in Maryland.

The trial

The EVITA trial was a cluster-randomized study run by researchers from Johns Hopkins, Emory and Yale in the US and the Federal University of Minas Gerais, Fiocruz and the World Mosquito Program in Brazil.

  • 58 school-defined clusters: 29 received releases of *Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes carrying the wMel strain of Wolbachia plus standard mosquito control; 29 received standard control alone.
  • 5,757 children aged 6 to 11 gave blood samples from 2020 to 2024, tested for antibodies to dengue, Zika and chikungunya.
  • Releases ran from January 2021 to May 2022.

The results

  • Overall protection against arbovirus infection: 68.6%.
  • Dengue: 68.5%. Chikungunya: 73.3%.
  • The trial overlapped with Brazil's largest recorded dengue outbreak, in 2024.
"The intervention substantially reduced dengue infections during Brazil's largest recorded outbreak." — Derek Cummings, Johns Hopkins

Wolbachia makes mosquitoes less able to pass viruses on, and once established it can spread through a local mosquito population on its own, without repeated spraying.

Caveats

These are early results from a conference presentation and a university press release. The team says they will be submitted to a journal for peer review, and the release did not include confidence intervals. The figures measure infections found by antibody tests in children, not symptomatic cases across whole communities, and Zika numbers were not reported separately. COVID-19 school closures also affected enrollment.

The work was funded about 83% by the U.S. National Institutes of Health and about 17% by Brazil's Ministry of Health.
