Devils Hole Pupfish Count Doubles to 137 After Near-Collapse in Death Valley

The National Park Service's October survey found more than twice as many of the world's rarest fish as a year earlier.

By the Happy News DeskNovember 3, 2026 · 2 min read

Photo: Presumably Olin Feuerbacher / Public domain via Wikimedia Commons

One of the rarest fish on Earth is having a better year. The National Park Service counted 137 endangered Devils Hole pupfish in an October 10, 2026 survey, more than double the 69 found in fall 2025 and nearly double the 77 counted in spring 2026.

What happened

The pupfish lives in a single water-filled limestone cavern in Death Valley National Park, California. Divers and surface counters from the park service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service checked the cavern's deep water and its shallow rock shelf, where the fish feed and spawn. The two agencies have used these methods since 1972.

Two earthquakes in 2025 sent waves through the cavern and disrupted the habitat, contributing to a steep decline. Biologists responded on several fronts:

  • Captive-raised fish: 72 were released into Devils Hole between March 2025 and February 2026
  • Extra food: teams supplemented feeding while the algae on the shelf recovered
  • Monitoring: guidance came from data collected at the Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility and from park service surveys
"It's exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year." — Mike Reynolds, Death Valley National Park superintendent

Why it matters

The species lives only about 10 to 14 months, so biologists believe most of the 137 fish were hatched and raised in the cavern itself rather than released. That suggests the wild population is reproducing again.

Still fragile

A single small habitat leaves the fish exposed. After an October 9 earthquake in Panama, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists saw early signs of stress in the pupfish, though a later visit found them in healthier condition. The park service says continued monitoring, research and habitat management remain essential for the species' long-term survival.
