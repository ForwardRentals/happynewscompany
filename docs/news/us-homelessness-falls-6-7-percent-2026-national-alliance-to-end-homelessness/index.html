U.S. Homelessness Fell an Estimated 6.7% in 2026, the Biggest Yearly Drop Since 2007

An independent analysis of national count data suggests about 49,000 fewer people were homeless on a single night, with veteran homelessness down nearly 8%.

By the Happy News DeskNovember 6, 2026 · 2 min read
National Alliance to End HomelessnessCommunity

Fewer Americans are sleeping in shelters and on the streets. On 6 November 2026, the National Alliance to End Homelessness reported that homelessness fell an estimated 6.7 percent between 2025 and 2026, which it describes as the largest single-year national decrease since 2007.

The numbers

The estimates come from an analysis of Point-in-Time Count data, a national snapshot taken on one night each year. The Alliance commissioned the analysis, which was carried out by Jared Schachner of the University of Southern California's Price School of Public Policy.

  • Total: about 693,000 people were homeless on a single night in 2026, roughly 49,000 fewer than a year earlier.
  • Sheltered: down an estimated 7.6%.
  • Unsheltered: down an estimated 4.9%.
  • Veterans: down an estimated 7.8%, and down about 60% since 2010.
  • Prior year: the 2025 decline was smaller, at 3.3%.

Honest context

Not everything improved. Long-term, or chronic, homelessness rose by an estimated 1%, which the Alliance links to a shortage of housing with support services for people who have been homeless longest. Total numbers also remain well above pre-pandemic levels.

Experts disagree about the causes. The federal housing department credits policies including border and encampment measures, while the Alliance points to housing programs that don't set preconditions and to softer rental markets. Alliance CEO Ann Oliva said her group's analysis found cities with encampment bans did worse than the national average. The federal department had not yet released its detailed report when the findings were reported.

What's next

Oliva cautioned that further progress isn't guaranteed, warning that proposed federal changes to homelessness funding could "erase these declines." Those changes have not taken effect, so they don't explain this year's drop. The Alliance's separate analysis found permanent housing exists for only about 9% of people experiencing homelessness, so communities still have far to go.

Source: National Alliance to End Homelessness
