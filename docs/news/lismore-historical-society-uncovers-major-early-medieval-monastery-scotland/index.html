A Small Scottish Island's Historical Society Uncovers a Major Early Medieval Monastery

Community-funded excavations on Lismore found burials, craft workshops and evidence of gold and silver working at the site of a sixth-century monastery.

By the Happy News DeskOctober 26, 2026 · 2 min read
Lismore Historical SocietyScience

Residents of Lismore, a small island in Scotland's Inner Hebrides, have helped uncover evidence that an early monastery there was a wealthy, influential centre of craft and learning, according to research published in the journal *Antiquity*.

The Lismore Historical Society launched the investigation, focused on the island's 13th-century cathedral. The monastery, Lios Mòr, is traditionally said to have been founded in the sixth century by Saint Mo Luóc, but until now little archaeological evidence confirmed what it was like.

What was found

Excavations led by Dr Clare Ellis of Argyll Archaeology revealed:

  • Burials: extensive burial grounds, with six burials dated between 650 and 1040 CE reported by Good News Network
  • Craft areas and early buildings, including an oval building with a limestone-paved foundation
  • Metalworking: moulds for casting penannular brooches and an early medieval touchstone used to test gold, plus debris indicating precious metals were worked on site

Prof. Gordon Noble of the University of Aberdeen, a co-author, described Lismore as "a thriving centre of worship, craftsmanship and learning." The authors suggest the monastery may have rivalled some of the best-known in early medieval Britain.

A community effort

Bob Hay, a former island resident, organised funding and volunteers. Island residents, who number roughly 170, crowdfunded the work according to Good News Network. Hay said the project shows "the value of community-led archaeology in advancing academic research."

Context

  • The "rivalling the best-known monasteries" conclusion is the researchers' interpretation of the finds, not a settled fact
  • The Antiquity article is free to read online (DOI 10.15184/aqy.2026.10379)

For a small island, the dig adds a major page to the story of early Christian Scotland.
