Student Groups Are Rescuing Usable Medical Supplies From the Trash

From an Ohio teenager's project to campus chapters in Michigan and California, young volunteers are redirecting sterile supplies to clinics that need them.

By the Happy News DeskOctober 9, 2026 · 2 min read
Project MedBridgeCommunity

Hospitals routinely retire supplies that are still sterile and usable, simply because of overstock or approaching dates. Reasons to Be Cheerful reports that student-run groups across the United States are intercepting some of that surplus and sending it where it is needed.

What the students are doing

In Ohio, 16-year-old Ben Kurian started Project MedBridge this year. In early August, a freight truck collected about 30,000 pounds of N95 masks and hot-and-cold packs from an OhioHealth facility in Columbus, an estimated $250,000 worth with expiry dates one to two years away. Shipping is handled by the Brother's Brother Foundation, and loads have reached maternal health facilities in Nigeria and front-line clinics in Ukraine. Kurian says the supplies are "perfectly usable, sterile."

  • United2Heal (University of Michigan): founded in 2007, with 100-plus members sorting donations every Saturday alongside World Medical Relief
  • Blueprints for Pangea: chapters from Ann Arbor to Los Angeles; the USC chapter says it has moved more than $2.2 million in items to about 14 countries since 2020
  • Pacemaker refurbishment: an FDA-approved effort with Michigan Medicine sends cleaned, reprogrammed devices to patients who could not afford new ones

Why it matters

The groups keep usable products out of landfills while filling real gaps in under-resourced clinics, and the students pick up skills in logistics, customs paperwork and nonprofit management along the way.

The challenges

The article notes that funding is limited, that some hospitals are wary of trusting teenagers with large donations, and that transport is a constraint for volunteers. Ocean freight can also take weeks.

The reporting is part of the outlet's "Born Into It" series, supported by UPIC Health.
