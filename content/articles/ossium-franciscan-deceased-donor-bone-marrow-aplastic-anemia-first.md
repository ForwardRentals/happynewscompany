Franciscan Health and Ossium Health announced on 10 November 2026 what they describe as the first reported transplant of bone marrow from a deceased organ donor to treat a non-malignant disease. The patient, a 40-year-old woman with severe aplastic anemia, is doing well six months later.

## What happened

Aplastic anemia is a rare condition in which the bone marrow stops making enough blood cells. The woman was diagnosed in 2015, and her disease worsened despite several therapies. She received the transplant on 18 April 2026 after a reduced-intensity conditioning regimen.

According to the companies, at six months:

- The donor marrow has engrafted durably.
- She no longer needs transfusions.
- Her blood and immune cells are entirely from the donor (full donor chimerism).
- No graft-versus-host disease has been reported so far.

> "To our knowledge, this is the first reported transplant of deceased organ donor bone marrow for severe aplastic anemia," said Dr. Felix Mensah, the treating physician at Franciscan Health.

## Why it matters

Doctors generally prefer marrow over circulating blood stem cells for non-malignant conditions, because it carries a lower risk of chronic graft-versus-host disease. But collecting marrow from a living donor requires surgery under anesthesia, so most unrelated donors give blood stem cells instead and marrow is in short supply. Ossium recovers and banks marrow from organ donors, which could offer another source. Its first deceased-donor marrow transplant, for a blood cancer patient, took place in June 2024.

## Context

This is a single patient with six months of follow-up, so long-term safety and durability are not yet established. The "first" claim comes from the companies themselves, and the announcement cites no peer-reviewed paper. Ossium says its HOPE Program plans to support more transplants for malignant and non-malignant diseases in 2026, which would show whether the approach holds up in more patients.
