A new kind of grid battery has come online in northwest China. A 60 MW, 600 MWh liquid-air energy storage plant in Golmud, in Qinghai province, was connected to the grid and began generating on October 30, according to trade outlet ESS News, which cites the developer.

The plant is run by state-owned China Green Development Investment Group (CGDG), working with the Technical Institute of Physics and Chemistry of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Construction began in July 2023.

## How it works

Instead of chemical cells, the system stores energy as very cold liquid. When wind and solar produce more power than the grid needs, the plant compresses air and chills it to about minus 194 °C until it becomes a liquid, which is held in insulated tanks. To get the power back, the liquid is warmed into gas, which spins turbines. Heat and cold released along the way are captured and reused.

- Rated power: 60 MW
- Storage capacity: 600 MWh, designed to discharge for about 10 hours
- Expected output: about 180 GWh a year, which CGDG compares to the use of roughly 30,000 households
- CGDG says all major equipment was made in China

## Why it matters

Solar and wind output rises and falls with the weather, so long-duration storage is one of the missing pieces of a clean grid. Liquid-air systems use no lithium and can be built from industrial components, and they can last through many cycles. Good News Network, which first flagged the project to us, describes it as the largest grid-connected plant of its type by power and capacity.

## Context

This is a demonstration project, and the reports do not give a cost or round-trip efficiency figure, two numbers that will decide how widely the technology spreads. Liquid-air storage is also being tried elsewhere, including a planned commercial project in Vermont in the United States.

If the Qinghai plant performs as designed, it will show that cold-stored air can fill the long gaps that short-duration batteries cannot.
