The world is making measurable progress against neglected tropical diseases, though not yet fast enough. That is the picture from the World Health Organization's Global Report on Neglected Tropical Diseases 2026, published on November 4, a mid-term review of the 2021-2030 road map.

## The good news

- As of June 2026, 63 countries had eliminated at least one of these diseases, up from 42 at the end of 2020. Togo became the first country to eliminate four, according to Health Policy Watch's coverage of the report.
- About 1.41 billion people needed interventions in 2024, a 19% drop since 2020.
- Sleeping sickness cases are down 98% from 1999 levels.
- Guinea worm disease fell to a record low of 10 human cases in 2025.
- Three indicators met their 2025 targets: chikungunya vaccine development, lower dengue case fatality, and wider availability of WHO-recommended antivenoms for snakebite.

The WHO's list covers about 21 diseases and disease groups, from river blindness and leprosy to dengue, rabies and snakebite envenoming. Many of them hit the poorest communities hardest.

## Why it matters

Each eliminated disease means children who no longer go blind, families who no longer lose income to illness, and health workers freed for other jobs. The report draws on 71 global indicators and input from more than 350 experts.

## Honest context

WHO calls the overall progress "uneven and fragile" and says the pace is insufficient to reach most 2030 targets. Lack of finance is the largest barrier, followed by conflict and displacement, climate change spreading mosquito-borne disease, weak surveillance and supply problems. In most endemic countries, treatment is not yet fully built into national health systems.

> "Sustainability has emerged as the defining constraint of our time," said Dr Daniel Ngamije Madandi, WHO's director for malaria and neglected tropical diseases.

The challenge for the next four years is to protect the gains already made while closing the funding gap.
