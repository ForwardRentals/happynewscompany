On summer nights, a small beetle's glow was once a familiar sight across Britain. Now a wildlife enthusiast working from a garden shed is helping it return to a corner of North Yorkshire, according to reporting by Good News Network, which draws on a Guardian feature.

## What is happening

Peter Cooper breeds glowworms in his spare time and releases the larvae at Nosterfield Nature Reserve, a roughly 600-acre complex of old limestone quarries that the Lower Ure Conservation Trust has reclaimed and rewilded. Glowworms had not been seen at the site for about 100 years.

- Cooper has counted about 1,900 larvae in his shed, due for release at Nosterfield this year
- Volunteers sometimes called "glow-getters" have built up expertise in rearing larvae in captivity
- Reserve manager Simon Warwick credits Cooper's "raw passion and enthusiasm," and says the insects are very rare in the region

## Why it matters

Glowworms were once abundant across Britain but have vanished from large areas, and the Guardian reports they are very hard to re-establish once gone. A reserve with wetlands, grassland and quarry habitat gives them a chance, and a reintroduction run largely by volunteers shows what small, patient efforts can do.

## What's next

It is early days. Success can't be confirmed until the offspring of released larvae mature and glow in the wild, which may not happen until around 2030. Until then the project is a hopeful experiment rather than a finished recovery.

For the volunteers at Nosterfield, that waiting is part of the work: raising each generation carefully and hoping a quarry that has been dark for a century will light up again.
