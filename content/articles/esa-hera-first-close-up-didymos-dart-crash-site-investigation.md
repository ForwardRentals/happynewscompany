The European Space Agency announced on November 11 that its Hera spacecraft has begun close studies of the asteroid Didymos and its small moon Dimorphos, the target NASA's DART probe deliberately struck in 2022. ESA has since released Hera's first close-up image of the pair.

## What Hera is doing

The car-sized spacecraft launched in 2024 and is now observing from about 25 to 30 kilometers away. Next month it is due to move closer, to roughly 20 kilometers. It carries 25 science instruments and two shoebox-sized CubeSats that will survey Dimorphos and try to land on it.

ESA's first image, taken with Hera's Asteroid Framing Camera, shows a side of Didymos that DART's camera never saw. It reveals the equatorial ridge, boulders, craters and possible grooves. Mission principal investigator Patrick Michel said it feels "like looking at a whole new world." Dimorphos is mostly in shadow in the image.

## Why it matters

DART hit Dimorphos on September 26, 2022, at about 6.1 kilometers per second and shortened its orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes, far more than expected. It was the first time humans measurably changed the motion of a natural object in the solar system.

- Dimorphos is about 151 meters across; Didymos about 780 meters
- The impact threw out rubble and boulders that added to the push
- Hera will measure the moon's mass and study the crater, so the technique can be better predicted for any real threat

## What's next

ESA says a bigger reveal with detailed images of both asteroids is planned for November 25. Neither asteroid poses a danger to Earth; the mission is about learning how deflection works, so that people are better prepared if a dangerous asteroid is ever found.
