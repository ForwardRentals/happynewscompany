Lake Baikal in Siberia, which holds about 20% of the world's surface freshwater, appears to be getting more water after a long dry stretch. A study in *Geophysical Research Letters*, led by Shijie Wang of Yunnan University, reports that river inflow has been well above its long-term average since 2017.

## What the researchers found

The team used tree rings from pines near the lake, along with an international tree-ring database, to reconstruct 343 years of flow in the Selenga River, the main river feeding Baikal.

- **Past decline:** reconstructed inflow fell through the 20th century, by up to 90% in the most severe periods
- **Recent turnaround:** since 2017, streamflow has been about 145% above the long-term average
- **Signs on the ground:** lake levels and vegetation growth are rising

## What may be behind it

The changes track moisture measures such as precipitation, snowmelt and soil wetness rather than local temperatures. The study links higher flows to cooler sea surface temperatures and more sea ice in the nearby North Pacific and Arctic, and suggests shifting weather patterns are bringing in more moisture.

## Honest context

The authors stress that the recent slowdown in Arctic warming reflects natural climate variability, not a significant shift in global warming. Increases in streamflow tend to be abrupt and short, while declines are gradual and long-lasting, and the recovery has lasted only about a decade. The links described are correlations.

Still, the finding is useful: the researchers say it can improve projections of lake levels and drying risk. They point to better water storage, wastewater treatment and restoring wetlands in the Selenga River Delta as ways to build resilience while the water is high. Baikal has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1996.
