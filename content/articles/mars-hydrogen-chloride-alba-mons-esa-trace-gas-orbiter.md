A European orbiter has spotted a recurring puff of gas above a Martian volcano that scientists think may be a sign of heat still stirring beneath the surface. The findings, published November 4 in the journal *Science Advances*, come from the European Space Agency's ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO).

## What the orbiter found

TGO first detected hydrogen chloride in Mars's atmosphere in 2021. On Earth, the gas often escapes from volcanic vents. On Mars it normally peaks in northern winter and fades by summer, but one place breaks the pattern: above fresh, narrow fissures on the flanks of Alba Mons, where spikes appear in early summer, in the same place, year after year.

- Camera images show cracks some hundreds of metres long, linked to changes in surface colour
- The team proposes that winter ice, at roughly -120°C, seals the cracks, and releases the gas as it sublimates in early summer
- The researchers examined other chemical explanations before concluding that a volcanic source is the best fit

## Why it matters

If hot magma sits beneath the surface, Mars may be more geologically active than many scientists assumed. Volcanic gases could also have helped thicken the planet's atmosphere and warm its climate in the past, and rising magma meeting groundwater could create warm, habitable niches.

> "These findings may prove to be TGO's greatest success yet." — Colin Wilson, ESA

## An honest caveat

The paper's own title calls the gas a "potential" signature of igneous activity. No new lava flows or eruptions have been observed, and the ideas about climate and habitability are possibilities, not conclusions. Co-lead author Daniel Mège of the Polish Academy of Sciences' Space Research Centre and colleagues say more observations will be needed to confirm the source.

Still, it is the kind of discovery that keeps planetary science exciting: a long-running orbiter, years of patient measurements, and a clue that a world often described as frozen and quiet may still have something going on underground.
