Scientists have shown that a whale's breath contains enough DNA to sequence its whole genome. A team led by researchers at the University of St Andrews in Scotland flew small drones through the exhaled spray of humpback whales in northern British Columbia, Canada, and published the results in *Molecular Ecology Resources*.

## How it worked

Sterile petri dishes were attached to a drone, which flew through the blow of one whale at a time. The team collected 58 samples from 26 individual humpbacks in the Kitimat Fjord System. More than a third of the samples were gathered by flying the drone from land.

- **DNA makeup:** about 84% came from the whale, and about 16% mainly from microbes in its airways
- **Repeat samples:** collected over two years from the same whales, and they gave matching results
- **Sex:** correctly identified for every whale

## Why it matters

Conservation genetics has often relied on skin biopsies taken with darts, which means approaching animals closely. The breath-based genomes gave genetic diversity estimates that closely matched those from traditional biopsies. The team also found three previously undescribed mitochondrial DNA variants and little evidence of recent inbreeding in this population.

Before this study, only one whole genome from a North Pacific humpback was publicly available.

Lead researcher Dr Éadin O'Mahony said the team will use the genomes to look at population structure and health, and also at kinship among whales feeding in the area. The researchers also note that a single breath may reveal information about the whale's respiratory microbes, a possible window into health.

## Partners and what's next

The work involved the University of Copenhagen, the North Coast Cetacean Society, Fisheries and Oceans Canada and the Gitga'at First Nation, in whose territory fieldwork took place.

This is a proof of concept on one population, so wider testing on other whales and sampling conditions will show how far the approach can go.
