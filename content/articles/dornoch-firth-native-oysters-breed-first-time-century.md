Native oysters are reproducing in the Dornoch Firth in northern Scotland for the first time in more than 100 years, Heriot-Watt University announced on 16 November 2026. Researchers found tiny baby oysters, known as spat, during routine monitoring of the Dornoch Environmental Enhancement Project (DEEP).

> "This is a moment over 12 years and 140,000 oysters in the making." — Prof. Bill Sanderson, Heriot-Watt University

## What happened

Oysters in the firth were probably fished to extinction in the late 1800s. DEEP, a 12-year partnership between Heriot-Watt, the Marine Conservation Society and whisky maker Glenmorangie, began in 2017 by placing 300 oysters from Loch Ryan on the seabed.

By October 2026, more than 140,000 juvenile oysters had been bred, hand-cleaned and released by scientists and volunteers. About 3,000 were reaching maturity across two established beds.

Research associate Gurjameer Ramday spotted the spat on shells placed on the seabed to attract them. Images were verified by invertebrate curators at National Museums Scotland and Amgueddfa Cymru – Museum Wales. On a return visit the young oysters had grown to roughly the size of a 50p coin, supporting the conclusion that the population is reproducing.

## Why it matters

Oyster reefs provide habitat for other marine life, capture carbon and help improve water quality, according to Sandy Luk, chief executive of the Marine Conservation Society, who called the result a historic milestone.

## What's next

The team plans to keep monitoring. The project's target is around 4 million oysters by 2031, which will require further funding, and the university has not given a count of spat or a growth rate for the new population. The project also aims to inform oyster restoration elsewhere in the UK.
