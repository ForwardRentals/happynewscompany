Everyday dog walks could one day help protect native habitats. Researchers at Germany's Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research (UFZ) report that ordinary volunteers and their dogs can be trained to find invasive plants, according to a study published on 4 November 2026 in *PLOS One*.

## How the study worked

A team led by Annegret Grimm-Seyfarth trained 31 dogs and their owners between 2021 and 2023 to detect three invasive plant groups: Himalayan balsam, common ragweed and the knotweed family, which includes Japanese knotweed. Training ended with a field test in the wild.

- **12 teams** completed the field test.
- **11 of those 12** passed.
- **75%** was the average detection success rate.

The project was funded by Germany's Federal Ministry of Education and Research.

## Why it matters

Catching invasive plants early makes them far cheaper and easier to remove, yet monitoring large areas is labor-intensive. Volunteer dog teams could add many extra noses, and give dog owners a way to contribute to conservation.

> "Reliable monitoring requires more than a good nose." — Annegret Grimm-Seyfarth, UFZ

## Caveats

This was a feasibility study, not a finished monitoring program. The authors stress that dependable results need good volunteer selection and coordination, solid dog training and expert guidance. They offer recommendations in those areas for early deployments.

## What's next

The findings suggest that, with the right support, volunteer teams could become a practical extra tool for ecologists, turning a favorite pastime into early warning for biodiversity.
