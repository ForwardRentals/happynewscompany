Scientists have published the first full portrait of two remarkably intact Pacific atolls, and the picture is a hopeful one. A study released on 11 November 2026 in *Frontiers in Marine Science* describes Bikar and Bokak, the northernmost atolls of the Marshall Islands, as healthy ecosystems from the sky to the deep sea. About 48,000 square kilometres around them have since been declared the country's first national marine sanctuary.

## What the survey found

Lead author Alan Friedlander, a marine ecologist at the University of Hawai'i, led fieldwork in 2023 as chief scientist of a National Geographic Pristine Seas expedition. The team used scuba, underwater video, deep-sea cameras and a submarine to look at birds, plants, turtles, reefs and fish.

- **Seabirds:** 15 colonies confirmed or likely to be breeding across the two atolls.
- **Turtles:** 132 green sea turtle nest pits on Bikar.
- **Fish:** at least 266 groups of reef fish, 79 groups of deep-sea fish and 15 groups of open-water predators such as sharks, jacks and wahoo.
- **New records:** 14 groups not previously recorded in the Marshall Islands, and two possibly new to science.

Bokak had more reef-fish biomass and coral-dominated reefs, while Bikar hosted most of the turtle nesting. The authors did not determine why the two differ.

## Why it matters

Because they are so remote, about 116 and 385 kilometres from the nearest inhabited atoll, and have been cared for for decades by the people of Utirik Atoll, the islands offer a rare baseline.

> "They are among the best ecological reference systems we have to better understand what we have lost elsewhere." — Alan Friedlander

The work supports the Marshall Islands' community-led *Reimaanlok* conservation framework. Friedlander also said the atolls are "not just treasures for the Marshall Islands but for the entire planet."

## What's next

The researchers recommend monitoring whole ecosystems rather than only shallow reefs, and say future work should study how nutrients and species move between habitats, which this survey did not examine. The study is a snapshot of conditions, so ongoing monitoring will show how the atolls fare as oceans warm.
