The World Wildlife Fund's 2026 Living Planet Report, published this week, carries a message the series has rarely delivered: conservation is working where it is tried, and the world has reached some positive tipping points.

> "Seemingly against the odds, we have a rational basis for hope." — WWF Living Planet Report 2026

## The comebacks

The report highlights species that have turned the corner:

- **Tigers:** wild numbers have risen by more than 70% over the past 15 years
- **African elephants:** many populations are stable or growing
- **Green sea turtles and Atlantic bluefin tuna:** both are no longer listed as Endangered
- **Sea otters:** numbers are increasing

[Al Jazeera's coverage](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/10/8/nature-can-recover-inside-the-race-to-rescue-worlds-crisis-hit-wildlife) adds mountain gorillas to the list. Tara Stoinski of the Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund told the outlet that the gorilla story shows "conservation can work."

## A tipping point in energy

Lead author Mike Barrett, WWF's chief scientific advisor, said the shift to clean energy has gone past the point of no return.

> "I would confidently say that we have passed a positive tipping point in the energy system." — Mike Barrett, WWF

Solar is now the cheapest source of new electricity in most countries, coal use is falling in many wealthy economies, and electric vehicles made up more than a quarter of new car sales in 2024, the report notes. Food systems and finance are earlier in the same journey.

## The honest context

The hope sits alongside a hard number: monitored wildlife populations fell by an average of 73% between 1970 and 2022. WWF's point is that the declines are not inevitable.

> "We can still end the loss of nature and indeed reverse it." — Mike Barrett, WWF
