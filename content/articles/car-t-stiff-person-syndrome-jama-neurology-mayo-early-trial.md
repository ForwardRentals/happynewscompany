Researchers at Mayo Clinic and two other sites have reported early signs that a single infusion of CAR-T cell therapy can substantially improve stiff person syndrome, a rare autoimmune neurological disease. The results were published in *JAMA Neurology* and reported on 9 November 2026. This is early research from a nonrandomized trial, not an approved treatment.

## What the study found

Stiff person syndrome causes progressive muscle stiffness and painful spasms, and standard therapies stop working for some people. The therapy tested, mivocabtagene autoleucel (miv-cel), reprograms a patient's own T cells to target the B cells driving the autoimmune attack. Participants received one treatment.

Mayo Clinic described "early evidence of substantial clinical improvement." Two patients illustrate the change:

- **Shari St. Germain**, whose symptoms began in 2021 and who stopped responding to standard care by 2024, was later able to walk on a treadmill again.
- **Jonathan Walden**, who needed help with walking and daily tasks, says he is back at work, hunting and fishing and has had no episodes since treatment.

> "The key question now is whether those improvements will be sustained over time," said Mayo neurologist Dr. Anastasia Zekeridou.

## Why it matters

No drug is currently FDA-approved specifically for stiff person syndrome. Separate one-year data from the 26-patient phase 2 study, presented in October by the University of Colorado Anschutz, found 92% of patients no longer needed immune-suppressing treatment for the condition. The therapy's developer, Kyverna Therapeutics, plans to complete a rolling Biologics License Application this quarter.

## Context

The trial had no randomized comparison group, patient accounts are individual experiences, and the treatment is not yet approved. Longer follow-up and regulatory review will show whether the benefit lasts and whether the therapy is safe enough for wider use. For people living with a disease that few doctors see, the results are an encouraging step.
