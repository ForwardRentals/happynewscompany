Antarctic blue whales are recovering. A study published November 5, 2026 in the journal *Endangered Species Research* estimates the Southern Ocean population at about 4,018 animals, up from roughly 418 around the time the last whaling ended in the early 1970s.

## How they counted

Lead author Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington, and colleagues built a single model that joins three kinds of evidence: historical catch records, decades of line-transect ship surveys, and photographs used to identify individual whales. That lets them reconstruct the population's whole path, from the whaling era to today.

## The numbers

- **Now:** about 4,018 whales
- **Low point:** about 418 in the mid-1970s
- **Growth rate:** an estimated 4.5% per year
- **Pre-whaling peak:** the study's model puts it above 200,000 in the early 1900s

An international ban on killing Antarctic blue whales came in 1964, though illegal Soviet whaling continued for about a decade afterwards. Rand describes the growth rate as realistic for a species that lives a long time and has one calf every two to three years.

## Why it matters, and what's next

The recovery shows that protection can work even for animals pushed close to the brink. Whales also shape ocean ecosystems; Rand has described them as "marine ecosystem engineers."

The picture is not all bright. The population is only about 1.9% of its pre-whaling size and the species remains listed as critically endangered. Few ship surveys have been done since 2004 because they are expensive, and scientists cannot yet turn recordings of whale song into total counts. Rand says continued monitoring across the Southern Ocean is needed to tell whether the whales keep rebounding as the climate changes.
