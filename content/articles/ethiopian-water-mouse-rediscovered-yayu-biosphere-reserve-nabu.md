A small, semi-aquatic rodent that science knew from a single specimen has been found alive. The Ethiopian water mouse, *Nilopegamys plumbeus*, was captured in mid-October during a biodiversity survey in Ethiopia's Yayu Biosphere Reserve, the German conservation group NABU announced on November 10.

## A species with a one-animal record

American zoologist Wilfred Hudson Osgood collected a single adult male in 1927, and the species was described in 1928. Despite later searches, no further records followed. The IUCN lists the species as critically endangered and possibly extinct.

The new find is in southwestern Ethiopia, several hundred kilometers south of the northwestern highlands where the first animal came from. Dr. Mengistu Wale of the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute caught it during a survey run from October 5 to 27.

## Who is behind it

- The survey was initiated and coordinated by NABU, with NABU Ethiopia
- Researchers from the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute and the Institute of Vertebrate Biology of the Czech Academy of Sciences are analyzing the data
- The project is funded by Germany's Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development

"This is an extraordinary find," Wale said. NABU's Yayu project lead, Katharina Kühnert, noted that knowledge of the species "has so far practically rested on a single animal."

## What happens next

Scientists are still evaluating the survey data. National Geographic reported that genetic tests were still underway, so the identification should be read as a rediscovery announced by the researchers rather than a finished scientific paper. The team hopes to learn about the animal's ecology and range, and to recommend long-term protection for the area and its species.

Rediscovery does not mean recovery: the mouse remains critically endangered until more is known. But after nearly 100 years, there is again a living animal to study.
