Archaeologists have identified 280 Palaeolithic engraved motifs in a cave in Catalonia, Spain, that tourists have been walking past for generations. The findings on Cova de la Font Major were announced on November 5, 2026, with the journal Antiquity.

The team, led by Josep Maria Vergès Bosch and Míriam Salas-Altès of the Catalan Institute of Human Paleoecology and Social Evolution (IPHES-CERCA), says it is the largest concentration of Ice Age art known in Catalonia, and the third-largest on the Mediterranean side of the Iberian Peninsula.

## Hidden in plain sight

The cave has been visited since the 12th century and was widely frequented from 1957. It became a tourist attraction in 1993. For more than six decades, visitors carved names and dates over the engravings without recognising how old they were. Its irregular, discoloured walls had led many to assume the cave was unsuitable for rock art.

- 280 motifs identified, according to the Antiquity statement
- Only two earlier Ice Age depictions were known in all of Catalonia
- Along the Iberian Mediterranean coast, about 75% of known sites have five or fewer animal figures, and over 90% have 20 or fewer

## Why it matters

The Iberian Peninsula holds more than half of the Upper Palaeolithic cave art documented in Europe, yet Catalonia had been a near-blank on the map. The motifs also differ from those of the Franco-Cantabrian region, which the authors read as evidence of a distinct Mediterranean tradition.

> "Contrary to what was previously thought, north-eastern Iberia holds first-rate Palaeolithic rock art sites," said Salas-Altès.

## Context

Good News Network reports that many of the engravings are shapes such as parallel lines and loops rather than recognisable animals, and that it also records dozens of animal figures. The Antiquity statement does not give a precise date for the engravings, so how old each one is remains to be worked out. The discovery is a reminder that important heritage can sit unnoticed in places people think they already know.
