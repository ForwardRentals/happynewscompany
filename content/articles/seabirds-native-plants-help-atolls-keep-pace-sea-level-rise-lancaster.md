Restoring seabirds and native trees on tropical atolls may help the islands build themselves up as seas rise, researchers led by Lancaster University reported on 14 October in *Science Advances*.

The team, led by Distinguished Professor Nick Graham, compared 28 atoll islands in the Seychelles, the Chagos Archipelago and French Polynesia's Tetiaroa Atoll. Some are rat-free with thriving seabird colonies; others are overrun with invasive rats.

## What they found

Seabirds feed at sea and bring nutrients ashore in their droppings, some of which wash onto the surrounding reefs.

- **Reef growth:** 1.8 times greater on lagoon reefs and 2.5 times greater on ocean-facing reefs around seabird-rich islands
- **Sediment:** an estimated 2.3 to 2.8 times more produced, the raw material that builds up island shorelines
- **Roots:** fine root length was an estimated 4.6 times higher in areas of native vegetation than where it was sparse
- **Scale:** for a 14-hectare island, roughly 170 cubic metres more sediment a year

Chris Perry of the University of Exeter called it "a sizeable uplift in the sediment production potential of these systems."

## Why it matters

Low-lying atolls are among the places most exposed to sea-level rise. The study suggests that removing invasive rats, bringing back seabirds and replacing abandoned coconut plantations with native forest could help some atolls keep pace with the sea-level rise projected under 2°C of warming. Coconut plantations are estimated to cover about 60% of Pacific atoll forests.

Jennifer Appoo of the University of the Seychelles said island restoration can enhance atoll resilience as well as benefit biodiversity.

## Context

- The figures are estimated potentials drawn from combined datasets, not results of new experiments
- Comparisons are between islands with and without seabirds and rats, so not every difference can be tied to a single cause
- Cutting greenhouse gas emissions remains essential

The findings point to restoration as a practical complement to climate action for island communities.
