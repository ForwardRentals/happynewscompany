Researchers at Monash University and the Zoological Society of London report that very small wildlife populations are far from hopeless. In a study published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* and announced on 5 November 2026, they found that most bird and mammal species that received direct conservation action after dropping below 50 wild individuals went on to recover.

## What the team found

The researchers examined every bird and mammal species known to have fallen below 50 wild individuals since 1950. Of those, 141 had enough tracked history to analyse, and 51 received direct help such as invasive-predator control, habitat restoration, captive breeding or translocations.

- **61%** of the species given direct action recovered to at least 100 individuals.
- Among species without direct support, **37% went extinct** and only **2%** reached 100.
- The best results came from combining threat reduction with hands-on population management.

One example is Australia's Lord Howe woodhen, which rose from 23 birds in the late 1970s to more than 1,600 today.

## Why it matters

A long-standing worry is that tiny populations are doomed by inbreeding and lost genetic diversity, which has sometimes pushed conservationists toward "triage" decisions that give up on the rarest species.

> "Population size alone shouldn't determine whether we give up on a species," said lead author Dr Donal Smith of Monash University.

Co-author Professor Rohan Clarke warned that abandoning critically endangered species because their numbers look too low would be a mistake.

## Context

The study does not say recovery is easy or guaranteed: many of these species remain vulnerable, and the success stories depended on sustained, often expensive work over years or decades. But it gives decision-makers evidence that investing in the smallest populations can pay off.

The research was led by Monash's School of Biological Sciences with researchers from the Zoological Society of London.
